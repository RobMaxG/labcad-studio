  1. PLA + water: hydrolyses — hot water/steam and prolonged aqueous contact break the ester backbone. Fine cold and brief.
  2. Nylon + water: not chemical attack — it absorbs water, swells and loses stiffness and dimensional accuracy. Death to a tight tolerance.
  3. DCM is a casting solvent for polycarbonate. It will wreck PC.
  4. PP shrugs off aromatics/chlorinated solvents cold, but swells in hot ones.
  5. Formic acid dissolves nylon outright (it's used as a PA solvent); concentrated mineral acids also degrade it.
  6. Strong base saponifies PLA's ester bonds — fast.
  7. Base hydrolyses polycarbonate — PC's big weakness, despite its heat resistance.

Which filament, at a glance

PP — polypropylene

Chemical champion

What your reagent bottles and Falcon tubes are made of. Near-bulletproof against acids, bases, ketones, alcohols. The catch: a pig to print — warps and won't stick. Worth taming for aggressive solvents.

Nylon (PA6 / PA12)

Best-printing broad resistance

Laughs at acetone, esters, ethers, aromatics. Enemies: acids, and its own water uptake — dry the filament, and don't trust it for wet-dimensional parts.

PETG

Aqueous-work sweet spot

Prints easily, handles buffers, alcohols and dilute acids fine. Keep it away from ketones and chlorinated solvents. My default for anything mostly-water.

PLA

Dry jigs only

Stiff and convenient, chemically the most fragile: esters, chlorinated solvents, base and even warm water get it. Great for fixtures and racks that stay dry; poor for wet chemistry.

ABS / ASA

Pick for heat, not chemistry

Broad organic-solvent weakness (acetone dissolves it). Choose for mechanical/thermal reasons; ASA adds UV resistance for outdoor/window use.

PC — polycarbonate

Strong & hot, chemically soft

High Tg (~145 °C) and tough, but dissolved by chlorinated solvents and hydrolysed by base. Great structural part, poor solvent barrier.

Three things that override the whole table

Autoclaving kills almost all of them

121 °C steam deforms PLA (~60 °C), PETG (~80 °C), ABS/ASA (~105 °C). PC and some nylons survive the heat but not necessarily the steam hydrolysis. Need sterile? Plan for chemical (EtOH/bleach) or gas sterilisation, or design for single-use.

FDM prints are porous

Layer lines wick solvent and trap contamination even when the bulk polymer is "resistant." A chemically-fine part can still be impossible to clean, may leach slowly, or harbour cross-contamination. Anneal, coat, or vapour-smooth anything that matters.

Colour & additives shift the answer

Pigments, plasticisers and fillers change chemical resistance and glass transition. Two spools of "PETG" aren't identical. For a critical part, soak a coupon of the exact spool before you commit the print.

General guidance for orientation, not a chemical-resistance spec — assembled from filament behaviour at the bench, room temperature, short-to-moderate exposure. Grade, colour, temperature and immersion time all move the line. For anything load-bearing, reagent-contact or long-immersion, verify with a coupon of the actual material.
